**From a curious student to a contributor, and the day we finally met in person**

I've been part of the Wikimedia movement for a little over a year, and on **2 October 2026**, the [Road to Wiki Cohort 2](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Road_to_Wiki/Cohort_2026) came to a close. We met our mentors and the community in person, and I realised how much more this program had given me than I expected when I joined. This is my attempt to write down what I did, who helped me, and what it left me with.

---

## 1. Where It Began

- I came in through **[WikiClub Tech](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/IIITH-OKI/WikiClub_Tech)**, part of [Open Knowledge Initiatives (OKI)](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/IIITH-OKI). It's a student-led platform meant to bridge academia and industry, so students get practical experience by contributing to Wikimedia through technology.
- My learning path was **[Road to Wiki Cohort 2026](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Road_to_Wiki/Cohort_2026)**. The program deliberately reaches Tier 2 and Tier 3 engineering colleges in India, and my own college, **United University**, is one of them. Its Wikimania 2026 proposal says Cohort 2 expanded to 8 colleges and 250+ new students.
- The reason it exists is a gap I lived myself. India has plenty of technical talent, but very few visible pathways from a classroom into a large open-source project like MediaWiki. Nobody had told us this was possible, and Road to Wiki did.
- Everything the program produces is openly licensed and documented on Meta-Wiki. That openness is what let me follow along, learn from earlier cohorts, and eventually add something back.

---

## 2. What I Actually Did Over the Year

- **Learned how the Wikimedia technical world works**: I started with the basics: how the projects fit together, what MediaWiki is, how contribution and review happen, and how decisions are made in public.
- **Took a session for the community**: I led a session on [Chai with Wiki](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Chai_with_Wiki/ta). Teaching what I had just learned showed me gaps in my own understanding and made the material stick.
- **Led as AI Lead of [WikiClub Tech UU](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/WikiClub_Tech_UU)**: As a member, and then in a leadership role, I helped my club think about how AI and open knowledge meet.
- **Built and deployed a tool**: I created **wikigo** and put it into the ecosystem:
  - Source: [github.com/saluja-ji/wikigo](https://github.com/saluja-ji/wikigo)
  - Listed on Toolhub: [toolhub.wikimedia.org/tools/wikigo](https://toolhub.wikimedia.org/tools/wikigo)
  - A type-safe Go SDK for the Wikimedia REST API wrapping the API with proper typing, context propagation, error wrapping, robust rate limiting, and backoff retry logic.
- **Stayed consistent**: Over more than a year I moved from *"what is this?"* to shipping something other people can find and use. That consistency taught me more than any single task.

---

## 3. 2 October: Meeting Everyone in Person

- After a year of calls, chats, and screen shares, we all gathered at **Bloom Hotel, Medicity, Gurgaon**.
- Putting faces to the names that had been guiding us was a different feeling. It turned a program I knew through a screen into a community I could shake hands with.
- Our mentors travelled to be there in person, with senior Wikimedia members, to meet us and talk about what comes next. That kind of time and attention isn't something students take for granted.

## 5. What This Journey Ultimately Gave Me

1. **Real, practical skills**: Learned to work inside a large, public, long-running codebase and community. Built and deployed **wikigo** from idea to published tool.
2. **Confidence, and a different idea of what's possible**: Showed that students from Tier 2 and Tier 3 colleges belong in large open-source projects.
3. **A community, not just a certificate**: Mentors and peers who know my work, who I can ask for advice, and who I want to keep learning with.
4. **A sense of purpose in my work**: Aligning with Wikimedia's mission to make knowledge freely available to everyone.
5. **A habit of giving back**: Returning as a mentor to help the next cohort of students.

---

## 6. What I'd Tell the Next Cohort

- Start before you feel ready, and ask questions in public. That's how everyone learns here.
- Build something small and ship it. A real tool teaches more than a pile of tutorials.
- Teach what you learn, even if only a few people attend.
- Talk to your mentors — they want to help, and they're more approachable than you expect.
